Speech Summary

Builders of Communion,
Not Babel

Pope Leo XIV — Encyclical on Artificial Intelligence & the Common Good, 2025

The Big Idea
Core Message

Humanity stands at a crossroads: we can use technology to build a new Tower of Babel — driven by pride, profit, and private power — or we can follow the example of Nehemiah rebuilding Jerusalem, working together under God to create a world of dignity, justice, and care for every person.

Technology is neither good nor evil by itself. It becomes what we make it. The Church calls everyone — believers and people of good will alike — to put the human person at the center of every choice, especially in the age of artificial intelligence.

Source: Encyclical — Pope Leo XIV, 2025
Key Arguments
1

Technology belongs to everyone, but today a few control it all. Private companies now hold more power over technology than most governments. This is new and dangerous. Rules and laws are needed, but rules alone are not enough — we must also ask who holds this power and how they use it.

§ 5
2

The Bible gives us two clear pictures of how to build society. Babel shows what happens when people build without God — they seek only power and a great name for themselves, and the result is confusion and division. Jerusalem under Nehemiah shows a better way: pray first, listen carefully, share the work, and trust God for the strength.

§ 7–9
3

Building for the common good rests on four pillars. First, root everything in a relationship with God. Second, accept human weakness — it is not a mistake to fix. Third, share responsibility; no one is too small to contribute. Fourth, speak with honesty and clarity — avoid both false excitement and unnecessary fear about new technologies.

§ 11–14
4

The Church's Social Doctrine is a living guide, not a rulebook. For 135 years, beginning with Pope Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum in 1891, the Church has listened to the world, learned from science, and offered wisdom rooted in the Gospel. It does not tell governments what to do; it helps all people discern what is just and what truly serves human dignity.

§ 3, 27–29
5

Every person has a "section of the wall" to rebuild. Scientists, workers, teachers, lawmakers, faith communities, and ordinary families each have a role. No one is so powerful they can do it alone, and no one is so small their contribution does not matter. This shared work — called subsidiarity — is the foundation of lasting peace and prosperity.

§ 13
Actionable Takeaways
▶

Be like Nehemiah — pray, plan, and then work. Do not be afraid of the hard work of our time. Start with prayer, think carefully, and then act with perseverance. Keep God first and the human person at the center of every decision.

Reject the "Babel syndrome." Watch out for three traps: (1) placing profit above people, (2) forcing everyone to be the same and erasing what makes each person unique, and (3) believing that data and algorithms can capture the full mystery of a human being. They cannot.

Choose the "way of Nehemiah" in everyday life. Practice dialogue instead of division. Include the poor, the sick, and the migrant in your plans — not as an afterthought, but as the foundation. Support digital literacy so that no one is left behind in the new digital world. Push for research and industry to serve justice, not just profit.

Remember: the "rejected stones" are the cornerstone. Pope Leo calls all of us — especially those with power — to remember that the people society most easily forgets are exactly the ones God places at the center. The poor, the sick, and the outcast are not problems to solve. They are the foundation of a truly good society.

Notable Rhetorical Devices

The Tower of Babel vs. the rebuilding of Jerusalem — two stories from Scripture used as one extended picture that holds the entire speech together. Babel stands for pride, uniformity, and godless ambition. Jerusalem stands for humility, diversity, and shared work with God at the center.

Extended Biblical Metaphor
Genesis 11 & Nehemiah 2–6 — §7–10

"The 'rejected stones' — the poor, the sick, the migrants and the least among us — will become the cornerstone, and a solid, welcoming common home will emerge on the earth."

Pope Leo XIV — Memorable Closing Image
Echoing Psalm 85:10 & the Cornerstone of Scripture — §16

"Never has humanity had such power over itself." A short, striking phrase that captures the danger of AI without using complicated language. It stops the reader and forces a serious question: if we have this much power, how will we use it?
